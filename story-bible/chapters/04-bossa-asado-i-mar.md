## Narrative

Before BOSSA was a restaurant, it was a Sunday. My family's asado wasn't an event we scheduled — it was a gravitational pull, the smell of fire and fish finding its way through the neighborhood until people who weren't technically invited showed up anyway with a drink in hand, because everyone understood the unspoken rule: there was always room for one more plate. I grew up believing hospitality wasn't a business category. It was just what a family did with its Sundays, its grief, its celebrations, its ordinary Tuesdays that needed an excuse to become something more.

Somewhere in my twenties, building AI tools during the week and still hosting that same gravitational Sunday, I started asking a question that felt almost disloyal at first: *what if this didn't have to be a once-a-week miracle? What if I built the room where it happened every day?* I'd grown up admiring the island's builders — local pioneers like Sevier Attaf, whose Pa Bou Real Estate built houses for people to live in. I started to wonder if I could build a table for people to belong at — asado i mar, fire and sea, the two things that had fed and shaped me longer than any code ever had.

It cost more than money. It cost the version of me that could keep every hour protected for consulting work, for the tidy, scalable, remote-friendly business I'd spent seven years building. A restaurant doesn't care about your calendar. It cares whether the grill is lit at 4 p.m. and whether the fish arrived fresh and whether the staff showed up, and it demands your physical presence in a way that no AI tool I'd ever built could substitute for. There were months where BOSSA took more from me than it gave back — broken equipment, a supplier who didn't show, a slow night that made me question the whole premise while I stood alone by the fire, watching coals glow orange against the dark water beyond the patio.

But something happened the first time a regular — someone with no obligation to say anything kind to me at all — told me the fish tasted like his grandmother's. I didn't build a system that day. I didn't ship a feature. I made someone feel, for the length of a meal, like the island remembered exactly who they were. That feeling doesn't scale the way a SaaS product scales, and for a long time I thought that made it a lesser kind of building. I don't think that anymore. I think it's the kind of building that reminds you why the other kind matters at all.

BOSSA taught me something about legacy that no consultancy client ever could: people forget dashboards. They remember tables. They remember who fed them the night something in their life fell apart, and who fed them again the night something in their life came together. My family taught me that land and a good name are the two things you protect first, and that both are measured in generations. I wanted our name to live on in a place people actually walked into, sat down, and stayed a while — smoke rising off the grill, the sea doing its evening color-changing performance just past the tables, family in the literal sense and family in the sense this island uses the word for anyone who's shared a table with you twice.

I still keep both lives running — the code at dawn, the fire at dusk. Some days that feels like two separate men sharing a body. Most days it feels like the most honest version of me there's ever been: an architect who understands that not everything worth building shows up in a codebase, and that the oldest technology on this island — fire, fish, and a table with room for one more — is still, somehow, the hardest one to master.
