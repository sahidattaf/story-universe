The Next 120 Hectares

The seawall at Boka Samí has not changed.

I want it to have changed. I want something to have happened here — a stone out of place, a new boat moored, a crack where none was before. But the seawall is the seawall. It was here before I was born. It will be here after. That is its whole point.

I have written three chapters from this spot. The first one was about a number — 120 hectares. The second was about land. The third was about a father.

This is the fourth.

I have been coming here for two years now. It is not for the view. I have better views in Kai Kòrsou. It is not for the quiet — there is no quiet in Curaçao at six in the morning, only quieter. It is for the moment.

The moment when the sun cracks the horizon.

The moment before anything has started.

I have built a lot in that moment. Software systems. Restaurants. Chapters. Agents. Threads. A village on paper. A protocol across four islands. A father's notebook full of decisions he never explained.

Two years. I have counted them. Two years ago I was a software man in a small office on Scharlooweg. Now I am a builder of things that go beyond software — a land company, a real estate vision, a network.

The number has changed.

Not 120 hectares. Something bigger. Something I cannot yet name.

I take out the notebook.

My father's notebook. The one he gave me in room 412. I have not written in it yet. Every time I open it I see his handwriting — small, careful, Papiamentu, 1988. Every entry is a purchase. Every entry is a decision. Every entry is a man building something he refused to call a legacy.

I flip to the last page.

There is nothing here. Just empty paper. It has been waiting for me.

I think about what to write.

I think about Melissa at the whiteboard, marker in hand, teaching me something I did not teach her. I think about Yolanda Croes in Aruba, forking the folder overnight. I think about Rùben fixing boats by day and building systems by night. I think about Jendrick's mother's clinic, now scheduled by an agent.

I think about the apprentices. The protocol. The islands.

I think about what I have not yet built.

I write one line.

I close the notebook.

I do not need to show anyone what it says. Not yet. Not for a long time.

The phone rings.

Melissa.

It is 6:42 in the morning. This is not a normal hour for her to call. Melissa calls at 9, or at 2, or at 11 at night. Never at 6:42.

I answer.

"Sahid." She is out of breath, the way she gets when she has been running. "I need you to listen."

"Okay."

"I built something. Last night. I couldn't sleep, so I built something."

"What did you build?"

She pauses. The pause is not hesitation. It is the pause of someone who has just realized what she has done.

"Kai Kòrsou has 120 hectares," she says. "Aruba wants 80. Bonaire wants 200. Sint Maarten wants a mixed-use development of about 60 hectares on the Dutch side."

"Okay."

"I built the folder," she says. "I built a Caribbean protocol for land. Not just content. Not just agents. Land. Development. Permits. Financing. Community input. Everything you did at Kai Kòrsou — but for all four islands. In one folder."

I do not say anything.

"Hello?" she says.

"I'm here," I say.

"It's rough. It's a first draft. But I think it works. I think — I think we could hand this to any island and they could use it. Any island. Not just the four."

I stand at the seawall. The sun has fully cracked the horizon. The water is bronze and gold. The boats are moving. Somewhere on the horizon, an island I cannot see is waking up.

I think about the number.

120 hectares used to be the biggest number I knew. It used to be the ceiling. It used to be the whole game.

It is not.

"Send it to me," I say. "I want to read it."

"I already sent it."

I look at my phone. There is a notification. A new folder has appeared in my Notion workspace. Its title is:

Caribbean Protocol — Land Edition

I open it.

Inside: four subfolders. Curaçao. Aruba. Bonaire. Sint Maarten. Inside each, an empty skeleton, waiting.

At the top, one file. It is a coordinator prompt. Melissa wrote it.

I read the first line.

It says: You are not building for the island. You are building with it.

I sit down on the seawall.

I do not need to say anything to her. She knows.

The next 120 hectares are not mine.

They are hers.
