## Narrative

I first heard the ladder laid out by Kevin Trudeau: seven phrases, each one a rung — *I wish. I want. I could. I can. I will. I am in the process of. I am.* Written down, it looks almost too simple. Lived out, it turned out to be the most accurate map of my life I've ever seen. Every chapter of this book sits somewhere on that ladder. I just didn't have names for the rungs while I was climbing them.

**I WISH.** The seawall at Punda. A boy watching pastel houses wake up across the water, wishing the island had the rooms he'd read about elsewhere. Wishing is where everyone starts, and there's no shame in it. It's desire admitting that it exists.

**I WANT.** The secondhand computer, the blackout nights. Wanting is sharper than wishing, and it brings company — every reason you shouldn't. *Who are you to want this? Nobody here does that.* Wanting is when the counter-voices first speak up. I heard them in Papiamentu, in Dutch, in English. They were fluent in all three.

**I COULD.** The early years of the build, underpriced and unseen, when the most I could honestly say was *maybe this is possible.* "Could" doesn't sound like much. But it's the first time you agree with the dream instead of arguing against it.

**I CAN.** This is the big jump, and it's where the old programming surfaces loudest. Saying *I can build AI tools from Curaçao* out loud meant confronting every quiet belief that talent has to leave to count. "Can" is where you discover what you were taught to believe about yourself. It's uncomfortable. It's supposed to be.

**I WILL.** Strangely, the future tense came easier. Once I believed I could, deciding became natural: I will open BOSSA. I will draft 120 hectares of coastline responsibly. "Will" is commitment without proof yet — the contract you sign with yourself before anyone else signs anything.

**I AM IN THE PROCESS OF.** This rung changed my life more than any other, because it can't be said honestly without action. You can wish from a hammock. You cannot be *in the process of* anything without evidence. That phrase is the engine room of Software 3.0 — specification, build, verification. It turns identity into work you can actually check. In Papiamentu it sounds even more like labor: *mi ta den proseso di* — I'm inside the process, hands dirty.

**I AM.** The final rung isn't loud. It's quiet, the way a tide is quiet. *I am a builder. I am of this island, not just from it.* By the time you can say "I am" truthfully, you're no longer trying to become something. You're describing what already happened while you were busy doing the work.

What I understand now, working with language models every day, is that words aren't only descriptions. They're instructions. In Software 3.0, the prompt becomes the architecture — the sentence you write determines what gets built. The seven words work the same way on a human being. Change the sentence you repeat to yourself, and you change what you'll build tomorrow. A vague sentence produces a vague life. A precise one produces a blueprint.

I also know the ladder isn't climbed once. Every new project sends me back to the bottom rung. Kai Kòrsou began as a wish all over again, long after BOSSA had reached "I am." That isn't failure. That's how growth works. You don't graduate from the ladder. You just get faster at climbing it, and less afraid of the rungs where the old voices live.

If you take one thing from this chapter, take the sixth rung. Wishing is free, and "I am" is earned — but *I am in the process of* is available to anyone, today, simply by starting. Say it only when it's true. Then make it true before lunch.

## Papiamentu — Resumen

Shete palabra a kambia mi bida: mi ta deseá, mi ke, mi por, mi ta kapas, mi lo, mi ta den proseso di, mi ta.
Kada palabra ta un stap di identidat — for di un mucha riba muraya di laman te un hòmber ku ta konstruí.
Idioma no ta djis deskribí realidat; e ta dibuha e blueprint promé ku e fundeshi.
