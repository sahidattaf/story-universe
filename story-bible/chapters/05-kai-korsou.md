# Chapter 5 — Kai Kòrsou

---

## Narrative

There's a story I heard long before I understood why it stuck to me — a real estate investor who, instead of asking "can I afford one house," asked "what if I bought the whole block?" I didn't grow up with the capital to think in blocks. I grew up on an island where most ambition, mine included, was sized to a single lot, a single building, a single Sunday's worth of dreaming. Kai Kòrsou is what happens when that ceiling finally breaks — not because the money arrived first, but because the thinking changed first, exactly the way it's supposed to.

120 hectares of coastline is not a number I say casually, even now. I say it the way you'd say a number that still occasionally wakes you up at 3 a.m., half in excitement and half in a very specific, very healthy fear. This isn't a restaurant I can staff myself if a shift falls apart. This isn't a piece of software I can patch at midnight. This is land — permanent, visible, generational — and every decision made on it will outlive the news cycle that eventually covers it, outlive most of the relationships involved in building it, quite possibly outlive me. You don't approach land like that with the same energy you approach a product launch. You approach it the way my ancestors approached the deeds that still carry the Attaf name: as something you are borrowing from the next generation, not something you fully own.

The audacity of it is not lost on me. A kid who once had to explain to skeptical early clients what "AI consulting" even meant is now standing on undeveloped coastline talking about marinas, eco-zones, and a beachfront that hasn't existed yet outside of renderings and my own stubborn certainty. People ask, reasonably, why an AI guy is building real estate. I tell them the truth: I'm not an AI guy who happens to build things. I'm a builder who happens to be fluent in AI. The medium changes. The instinct — see an unfinished thing, imagine its finished shape, refuse to wait for permission to start sketching it — has been the same since I was a boy explaining code to a computer nobody else in the house understood.

What makes Kai Kòrsou different from anything before it is the word *eco* sitting right next to the word *zone*. This island raised me on water that changes color at dawn, and I refuse to build something that trades that performance away for a faster return. Every version of the plan gets tested against one question: does this still let the water do what it's always done, or does it quietly ruin the thing that made this coastline worth developing in the first place? That question has cost me speed. It has cost me investors who wanted a simpler yes. I've kept asking it anyway, because a legacy built on a ruined coastline isn't a legacy — it's a withdrawal, dressed up as a deposit.

I stand on that land more often than the spreadsheets require, because the spreadsheets don't capture the actual weight of it — the wind off the water at a certain hour, the exact angle where the future marina will meet a shoreline that has belonged to fishermen's grandchildren for longer than anyone can properly account for. I owe them the version of this that respects what was here before my name was ever attached to it.

The boy on the seawall in Chapter One asked whether the horizon was a limit or a blueprint line. Kai Kòrsou is the most literal answer I will ever give that question — 120 hectares of horizon, finally being drawn instead of just watched.

## Papiamentu — Resumen

Kai Kòrsou ta e pensamentu di "kumpra henter e blòki" aplika riba 120 hektar di kosta. E no ta un proyekto pa hasi plaka lihé — e ta un erensia ku mester respetá e awa i e piskadónan ku tabata aki promé ku kualke plan. Orizon no ta un límite; e ta un blueprint ku mi ta dibuha awor.
