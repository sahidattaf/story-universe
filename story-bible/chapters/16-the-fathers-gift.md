1. FULL NARRATIVE
The Father's Gift

AdventHealth Curaçao has a wing where the windows face west.

That is not a design accident. Someone, years ago, understood that the people in those beds are mostly older, that they are mostly Curaçaoan, and that the last thing a Curaçaoan wants to see before sleeping is anything other than the sea. So the windows face west. The sunsets come in without asking.

My father is in room 412.

He is seventy-eight. He is not dying — the doctors are careful to say this, twice — but he is slowing. His heart is doing the thing hearts do when they have been running on island time for eight decades. He had a small scare last week. He is here for observation. He will be home by Friday.

But something in the way he called me — the exact tone, the pause before the request — told me this was not about Friday.

I drove over at seven in the morning. I brought nothing. He did not ask me to bring anything.

When I walk in, he is sitting up, wearing a linen shirt that he clearly had someone iron. Even in a hospital bed, my father irons his shirts.

"Sahid," he says. Not a question. Not a greeting. Just the name, the way he has always said it — as if saying it were the whole sentence.

I sit in the chair next to him. There is a small table. On the table, a leather notebook.

He sees me see it. He smiles.

"You remember this," he says.

I do not. Or rather — I remember the shape of it. I remember seeing this notebook on his desk, on the dash of his car, in the drawer of the ATTAF REAL ESTATE office on Pietermaai when I was a boy. I never opened it. I never asked. I assumed it was a ledger. I assumed it was business.

"It is not business," he says, reading my face the way he always has. "It is not a ledger. It is a book."

He opens it.

The first entry is dated 1988. He was forty years old.

The handwriting is small, careful, and Papiamentu.

"Awe mi a kumpra 4 hectárea na Westpunt. E terser hende a bisa mi ku e tera no ta bal nada. Mi no a kere."

He turns the page slowly.

"Every deed I ever bought," he says, "I wrote it here. The hectares. The price. The seller. The day. And one line about what I was thinking, or afraid of, or hoping for."

I look at the pages. There are hundreds of them. He has filled three notebooks. This is the fourth.

"Forty years," I say. I do not mean it as a question.

"Forty years," he says. "And every one of them I thought I was building a business. But I was not."

He stops. He looks at the window. The light is doing that thing it does around eight in the morning — soft, bronze, unhurried.

"There was a moment," he says, "when I almost sold Kai Kòrsou."

I do not answer.

"I was tired," he says. "I was fifty-six. Your mother was sick. The bank was asking questions I did not want to answer. A developer from Miami came to me with a number that would have solved everything."

He pauses.

"I had the contract in front of me."

"And?" I say. It is the only word I can find.

"And I didn't sign it," he says. "Not because I was brave. Because I looked at the deed, and I looked at the map, and I thought — if I sign this, my son will never stand on this land."

He turns to me.

"That is the whole reason. It was not strategy. It was not legacy. It was not business. It was you."

I do not know what to say. I have spent my whole adult life telling the story of how I built things — how I assembled projects, chapters, systems, threads. And here is a man who built a single decision, forty years ago, because of a sentence he could not say out loud.

"You gave me Kai Kòrsou," I say finally. "That is not a small gift."

He shakes his head.

"No," he says. "I did not give you Kai Kòrsou. The land is not a gift. The land is just dirt with a deed attached."

He closes the notebook. He puts his hand on it.

"What I gave you," he says, "is the courage to hold it. That is the gift. The land was the receipt."

He hands me the notebook.

It is heavy in a way that paper should not be. Or maybe it is not the paper at all. Maybe it is what forty years of small careful handwriting weighs.

"Take it," he says. "I am not finished writing. But you need to know it exists."

I hold it for a long time. I do not open it. I do not need to. I understand what it is now. It is the proof — the paper trail of every decision my father made that did not make money, and did not make headlines, and did not make sense to anyone outside the family.

It is the paper trail of the man who almost sold the land, and then did not, because he imagined a son on it.

I take a printed page from my bag. Chapter 13. "The 120 Hectares." I hand it to him.

He reads it. Slowly. The way he reads everything.

When he finishes, he is quiet for a long time. Then he says, in Papiamentu, in a voice that is doing something I have never heard it do before:

"Bo ta skirbi mi mihó ku mi mes."

You write me better than I write myself.

I do not answer.

There is nothing to answer to that.
